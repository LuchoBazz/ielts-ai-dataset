# Section Profiles

Detailed conventions for each IELTS Listening section. Read the profile for the section you are generating before drafting. Each profile lists: purpose, speakers, discourse features, distractor style, data conventions, default temperature, and example lines.

## Section 1 — Everyday conversation

**Purpose:** A transactional exchange in a social/everyday context — booking, enquiry, registration, application, arranging a service. This is the classic "form-filling" section.

**Speakers:** 2. Usually an official/agent (Speaker 1) and a member of the public/customer (Speaker 2). One of each gender works well for voice contrast.

**Register:** Polite, cooperative, transactional. Full sentences, courteous fillers ("Certainly", "No problem", "Let me just check").

**Discourse features:**
- The agent drives the exchange, asking for details one at a time.
- Frequent confirmation/read-back ("So that's... is that right?").
- Data revealed in a logical intake order: name → contact → address → dates → specifics → price → payment.

**Data conventions (critical):**
- **Spell surnames** letter by letter, ideally with a clarification ("H-A-R-R-I-S-O-N — Harrison with two R's? — no, one R").
- Read **phone numbers, prices, dates, times, and reference codes** aloud the way people actually say them.
- Place at least one **distractor** right beside a key datum.

**Distractor style (medium default):**
- Self-correction of a number or date: "the 15th — actually, make that the 14th".
- Wrong-then-right choice: "by card — oh, no, I'll pay by bank transfer".
- Mishearing corrected: "Was that fifty? — Fifteen. One-five."

**Default temperature:** 0.7
**Pace:** Slow to Natural. **Articulation:** Very clear.

**Example lines:**
```
Speaker 1: Certainly. Could I take your surname first?
Speaker 2: It's Buchanan. B-U-C-H-A-N-A-N.
Speaker 1: B-U-C-H... A-N-A-N. Lovely. And a contact number?
Speaker 2: It's oh-seven-nine-double-four, three-one-two, eight-oh-six. Sorry — eight-six-oh.
Speaker 1: ...ending eight-six-oh. Got it. And the total today comes to forty-five pounds — no, I beg your pardon, fifty-four.
```

## Section 2 — Everyday monologue

**Purpose:** One person giving practical information to a general audience — a guided tour, a facilities briefing, a local radio slot, event arrangements, a "what's on" talk.

**Speakers:** 1.

**Register:** Informative, warm, semi-formal. The speaker is a guide, host, coordinator, or presenter.

**Discourse features:**
- Strong **signposting**: "Let me start by...", "If you look to your left...", "Moving on to...", "One last thing...".
- Orientation language for maps/plans: directions, relative position ("just past the", "on the far side").
- Enumerated arrangements (times, locations, do's and don'ts).

**Distractor style (medium default):**
- Change of plan mid-talk: "The talk is usually in Hall B — but today it's been moved to Hall D."
- Exception to a rule: "Photography is fine everywhere — except the orchid house."

**Default temperature:** 0.8
**Pace:** Natural. **Articulation:** Clear, engaging.

**Example lines:**
```
Speaker 1: Good morning everyone, and welcome to Ashford Wetlands Centre. Let me start with a few practicalities. The café is open from nine, though hot food isn't served until eleven. Now, if you look at the map on your leaflet, the main hide is straight ahead past the pond — not the smaller one to your right, that's staff only. Moving on to today's guided walk...
```

## Section 3 — Academic discussion

**Purpose:** A discussion in an educational/training context — a tutor and student(s) reviewing an assignment, planning a project, discussing a reading, or getting feedback.

**Speakers:** 2 to 4. Common: one tutor/supervisor + one or two students. Give each a distinct voice.

**Register:** Academic but collegial. Hedging, evaluation, agreement and polite disagreement.

**Discourse features:**
- Turn-taking with genuine interaction: building on, questioning, qualifying.
- Hedged opinions: "I suppose", "I'm not sure I agree", "that's a fair point, but".
- Comparing options and reaching decisions — decisions are often where the answers sit.
- Occasional overlap or interruption (mark with an em dash), but keep it intelligible.

**Distractor style (medium–heavy):**
- A view proposed, then revised after discussion: "Let's use the survey data — actually, the interviews will be stronger."
- Two students disagree; the resolution is the answer.
- Options considered and rejected before the chosen one.

**Default temperature:** 0.9
**Pace:** Natural, slightly quicker. **Articulation:** Clear despite the back-and-forth.

**Example lines:**
```
Speaker 1: So, for the methodology, I was going to just hand out a questionnaire.
Speaker 2: Hmm. A questionnaire would give you numbers, but for something this exploratory —
Speaker 1: You think interviews?
Speaker 2: I'd lean that way, yes. Fewer participants, but far richer data. Say, six to eight.
Speaker 1: Right, six to eight. Okay, interviews it is, then.
```
(Speaker 1 = student, Speaker 2 = tutor, for example.)

## Section 4 — Academic lecture

**Purpose:** A single-speaker academic lecture or talk on a study-related subject (science, history, environment, social science, technology).

**Speakers:** 1.

**Register:** Formal academic. Precise vocabulary, complete arguments, no chit-chat.

**Discourse features:**
- Clear macro-structure: introduction of topic → points in sequence → examples → conclusion.
- Discourse markers: "First", "A further factor", "As a consequence", "To summarise".
- Definitions, cause-and-effect, contrast, exemplification.
- Denser, lower-frequency vocabulary than other sections — but always clearly enunciated.
- **No interruptions or self-correction distractors** of the Section-1 kind. Difficulty here is lexical and structural, not misdirection. (A lecturer may still reframe: "or rather", "more precisely".)

**Default temperature:** 0.85
**Pace:** Natural, measured. **Articulation:** Precise, authoritative.

**Example lines:**
```
Speaker 1: Today I want to examine how cities are adapting to rising summer temperatures. Now, the most visible strategy is what's called the urban forest — simply, planting trees at scale. Trees cool a street in two ways: directly, through shade, and indirectly, through a process called evapotranspiration, whereby water released from leaves lowers the surrounding air temperature. A further factor, often overlooked, is surface albedo — the reflectivity of roofs and pavements...
```
